Design Criteria for Cross-Chain Token Swaps

3.3 Design Criteria for Cross-Chain Token Swaps

Having surveyed the landscape of existing swap protocols in the previous section, this section establishes the concrete design criteria that any cross-chain token swap mechanism must satisfy to function reliably within our bonding curve interswap stock market on Monero. These criteria translate the strengths and weaknesses identified earlier into actionable engineering constraints, balancing Monero's privacy guarantees against the practical requirements of atomicity, liquidity, and usability.

3.3.1 Atomicity and Trust Minimization:

A cross-chain swap must be atomic: either both legs of the exchange complete, or neither does. Partial execution, where one party transfers value and the counterparty fails to reciprocate, is unacceptable in a trust-minimized system. For Monero specifically, this points toward adaptor-signature-based atomic swaps or hash time-locked contracts (HTLCs) adapted to work with Monero's ring signatures and stealth addresses, since Monero does not expose a general-purpose scripting layer the way Ethereum does.

  • Adaptor signatures: Enable atomicity by binding the release of a secret on one chain to the validity of a signature on another, without requiring on-chain scripts, making them naturally compatible with Monero's minimal scripting model.
  • Timeout and refund paths: Every swap must include a well-defined timeout after which an uncompleted swap can be safely refunded to its originator, preventing funds from becoming permanently locked.

3.3.2 Preserving Monero's Privacy Guarantees:

A swap design that leaks the linkage between a Monero output and a counterpart asset on another chain undermines the very reason for building on Monero. Design criteria here include minimizing on-chain metadata correlation, avoiding designs that require revealing view keys to third parties, and preferring off-chain negotiation (only the minimal cryptographic commitments should touch the base layer). Where possible, swap amounts and timing should be decorrelated from typical wallet behavior to resist timing-analysis and amount-correlation attacks.

3.3.3 Liquidity and Price Consistency with the Bonding Curve:

Because swapped tokens ultimately interact with the bonding curve described in Chapter 2, the swap mechanism must not introduce arbitrage windows that destabilize the curve's pricing function. This requires:

  • Bounded execution latency: The longer a swap takes to settle, the larger the price drift risk between the source chain price and the bonding curve price at settlement time. Design criteria should target settlement times short enough to keep slippage within the curve's tolerance band.
  • Oracle-free or minimally-trusted pricing: Wherever feasible, the swap rate should be derived from the bonding curve itself or from decentralized price feeds, rather than a single centralized oracle, to avoid introducing a new trust bottleneck.

3.3.4 Usability and Fee Predictability:

A protocol that is cryptographically sound but operationally unusable will not see adoption. Design criteria include predictable, bounded fees (accounting for Monero's dynamic block-size fee market), clear failure modes that are surfaced to the user rather than silently stalling, and wallet-level tooling that abstracts away the multi-step handshake typically required by atomic swap protocols.

3.3.5 Security Under Partial Chain Failure:

Since the swap spans two independent consensus systems, the design must remain safe even if one chain reorganizes, stalls, or experiences a fee spike during the swap window. Criteria here include conservative confirmation depth requirements on both chains and explicit handling of the case where a counterparty is unresponsive, ensuring funds default to the safe (refundable) state rather than an ambiguous one.

Together, these five criteria (atomicity, privacy preservation, price consistency with the bonding curve, usability, and resilience to partial chain failure) form the specification against which the concrete swap implementation in the following section is evaluated.

Implementing Token Swap Functionality on Monero

Chapter 3: Token Interoperability and Swap Protocols

3.2 Implementing Token Swap Functionality on Monero

This section details the implementation of token swap functionality within the Monero ecosystem, specifically tailored for our bonding curve token interswap stock market. This involves leveraging Monero's inherent privacy and security features, while addressing the complexities of token interoperability and exchange.

3.2.1 Monero's Role in Token Swap Security and Privacy:

Monero's native cryptographic design is crucial for ensuring the security and privacy of our token swap protocol. Key features contributing to this include:

  • Ring Confidential Transactions (RCT): RCTs are fundamental to concealing transaction details. Within our swap protocol, the exchange of tokens will leverage RCTs to prevent the tracking of individual participants and the associated token quantities. This is particularly important to maintain the privacy of both buyers and sellers of tokens.

  • Stealth Addresses: Our system will utilize stealth addresses for all token swap transactions. This prevents the need for revealing public addresses to perform exchanges, protecting user anonymity and simplifying on-chain interactions. The use of Monero's stealth address system allows the recipient of tokens to conceal their identity from those with whom they aren't interacting directly.

  • Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs): The validation of swap transactions might utilize ZKPs to verify certain aspects without revealing sensitive information. For example, ZKPs can prove that a valid amount of tokens was transferred, or that the correct bonding curve parameters were met, without revealing the exact amounts or parameters.

  • Simplified Payment Verification: Utilizing existing Monero tools and libraries for transaction verification will expedite the process, reducing complexity and improving efficiency. This aspect of implementation will be detailed in the following sections, along with the necessary code snippets.

3.2.2 Architecture of the Swap Protocol:

The token swap protocol will be structured in several key modules:

  • Token Registry: This module maintains a decentralized, immutable ledger of all supported tokens. It will contain information about each token, including its unique identifier, its associated bonding curve parameters, and any necessary escrow information. The registry's immutability is critical for maintaining trust and preventing manipulation.

  • Swap Agent: This agent acts as the intermediary during the swap process. It takes orders from buyers and sellers, verifies the parameters of the swap against the token registry, and facilitates the transfer of tokens using Monero's RCT functionality.

  • Order Book Management: A specialized module is required to handle order book management. This module will facilitate the matching of orders based on the current market conditions dictated by the bonding curve. Crucial aspects include the ability to manage orders efficiently, ensuring the order book's consistency and functionality.

  • Bonding Curve Integration: The swap protocol must seamlessly integrate with the chosen bonding curve model. This involves ensuring that the bonding curve's supply and demand are accurately reflected in the order book and that transactions are properly calculated and executed based on the current market rate. The protocol should ideally automatically adjust to changes in the bonding curve's parameters.

  • Escrow Mechanism: An escrow system will be necessary to maintain the integrity of the swap. Funds will be held in escrow accounts until the transaction is validated, thus preventing fraud and manipulation. This will incorporate the necessary cryptographic mechanisms for securing the escrowed tokens.
